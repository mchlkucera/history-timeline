A map of how a technology spreads

“See how printing spread, or gunpowder, or the textile loom, or computing.” Four diffusions, hand authored against conventional scholarship, on the app’s own atlas and under the app’s own time rail. Drag the scrubber and the picture is the state of the spread in that year: what had arrived, where, and along which road.

The corpus already has these four as spreads — a weight curve and a moving footprint, which is when and how big. It has no where-next. So this page has two jobs: show what a diffusion map feels like in the app’s language, and pin down the smallest data shape that would carry one, so that a later ETL has something to aim at. Everything the map is wrong about, it is wrong about out loud.

The base map is the app’s own atlas — web/public/data/worlds.json, eight snapshots of it, simplified in build-data.py and re-encoded in the atlas’s own delta format, then decoded here by a copy of render/shared.ts decodeSnapshot(). The borders step to the snapshot nearest the index year, the way MapView.syncToYear() already does. Internal borders are drawn at low contrast on purpose: on this page the polities are ground, not figure.

The data shape

The starting point held. Four fields were added, and every one of them was added because a real row would otherwise have had to lie.

diffusion = {
  id, name, cat,          // cat is one of the corpus's eight; it colours every mark
  spread,                 // the relations.json spread this corresponds to, or null
  unit,                   // ADDED. what is actually spreading. one sentence, shown to the reader
  claim,                  // ADDED. what "arrival" means FOR THIS DIFFUSION. one sentence, shown
  span: [y0, y1],
  origin:     { place, year, note, contested? },
  precursors: [{ place, year, note }],            // ADDED. older than the origin, no path
  hops:       [{ place, year, via?, from?, note?, contested?, route?, fate? }]
}

place = { id: { name, lat, lon, src: 'corpus' | 'hand', key? } }

via      'trade' | 'conquest' | 'migration' | 'print' | 'institution' | 'copy'
         drawn as a GLYPH on the arc. Never a colour (the eight category hues are
         the corpus's data system) and never a dash (tokens.css already spends
         dash on confidence). 'copy' was ADDED: Slater, Bauwens, Lowell and the
         Soviet System/360 clone are not trade, not migration, not conquest.

from     the place the arc is drawn FROM. ABSENT = an arrival with no drawable
         parent — which on this map is a claim, not a gap. Mainz 1450 has no
         `from` and that is the single most important cell in the file.

contested   the DATE is argued about  → the arrival dot is a slashed ring
route       the PATH is inferred      → the arc is dashed
            ADDED. Gunpowder forced these apart on its first European hop:
            Florence 1326 is a rock-solid date on a completely unknown road.

fate     'faded' — it arrived, it ran, and it stopped. ADDED for Nagasaki 1590,
         Muhammad Ali's Cairo mills, Müteferrika's press, Colossus. Four of the
         four diffusions needed it, and no weight curve can say it per place.

Small enough to fill: — hops across four diffusions, at — places, of which — came straight out of the corpus’s PLACEMAP by name and the rest had to be typed. The generator validates every row on build — that every from resolves, that no parent is younger than its child, that every via is in the vocabulary — and it currently reports — problems, having reported seven the first time it ran. Those seven are in the section below.

What authoring the four actually taught

One paragraph each. The interesting output of this prototype is not the map; it is the list of things the map could not say until a field was added.

Printing

It proved the shape, and it broke the from field on the most famous row in the file. Kaifeng 1040 → Ganghwa 1234 → Cheongju 1377 is a real chain and draws beautifully. Mainz 1450 is not on it. No evidence of transmission from East Asia has ever been produced and the field treats Gutenberg as independent, so the honest hop has no from at all — and the map has to render an arrival with no arc as a deliberate statement rather than as missing data. Then Istanbul: the press arrives in 1493 with Sephardic refugees printing Hebrew and again in 1727 with an imperial licence to print Arabic script — two arrivals, same coordinates, 234 years apart, and the second one closes in 1742. One place, several arrivals, one of them fatal. Any shape that keys arrivals by place is already wrong.

Gunpowder

It split contested in two and then broke chronology. Florence, 11 February 1326, is one of the firmest dates on this page — a municipal order for brass cannon — sitting at the end of a road nobody can name. A doubtful date and a doubtful route are different doubts and need different marks, so route was added and the arc is dashed while the dot stays solid. Then Niebla 1262 broke the tree outright: its natural parent, al-Rammah at Damascus, is dated 1280 — eighteen years after the child. That is not a data error; it is what happens whenever you build a tree over “earliest surviving evidence”, because evidence and transmission are not the same sequence. The build script caught it, and the fix (re-parent to Baghdad 1258) is exactly the silent lie an ETL would tell.

Mechanised spinning & weaving

It forced via: copy and fate: faded, and it showed that two diffusions can run on one map at different speeds. Slater memorised Arkwright and sailed under a false trade; Bauwens smuggled mule-jennies and the men to run them; Lowell toured Lancashire and rebuilt the loom from memory. None of that is trade, migration or conquest — the transfer has no willing sender, and the vocabulary needed a sixth word. Cartwright’s 1785 power-loom patent is the loudest trap on the page: the machine did not work, and weaving does not actually mechanise until Roberts in 1822, so a patent date used as an arrival date would put the dot 37 years early. Cairo is the clearest faded: thirty state mills by the 1830s, gone after the 1841 settlement stripped the tariff. And Bombay 1854 arrives twenty-five years after Lancashire yarn had already destroyed the Indian handloom’s export trade — the goods diffused decades ahead of the machine, and this shape can only draw one of the two.

Computing

It broke the shape three times and it should be shipped broken. The origin is a design — Babbage 1837, never built — and a design cannot spread the way a press can. Then Zuse, Atanasoff and Lebedev all arrive with no parent, because they genuinely had none. Then Bletchley: Colossus worked in 1944 and transmitted nothing, classified until 1975 — a node whose outgoing arcs were legally forbidden to exist, which is one of the main ways a technology fails to spread and which no diffusion shape models. Then the unit changes under you: a machine (1941–51), a component (1947–71), a protocol (1969–93). By Mosaic in 1993 the thing arrives everywhere a wire goes, at once, and “printing reached Venice in 1469” has no 1995 equivalent. The map stops at 1993 and says why. That is the honest rendering, and it is better than faking a dot in every capital.

What an ETL would need, and how bad the data is

Honest answer first: there is no “arrival of technology X at place Y” predicate in Wikidata, and there never has been. The nodes are partly recoverable. The arcs are not recoverable at all.

How bad, per diffusion, guessing honestly

  • Printing 1450–1500 in Europe: near-perfect, and not from Wikidata. The Incunabula Short Title Catalogue and the Gesamtkatalog der Wiegendrucke record the first press in roughly 280 European towns with dates and imprints; Iowa’s Atlas of Early Printing is literally this map already. A loader against ISTC would fill the European half of the printing diffusion better than a person could, in an afternoon. Outside 1450–1500 and outside Europe, it collapses to zero.
  • Computing: about half. Early machines are unusually well curated — an item per machine, with P571, P276 and often the lab. You would get Z3, ENIAC, SSEM, EDSAC, MESM, the ICs and the microprocessors. You would get none of the arcs, and the ones that matter most (the EDVAC report; the 1973 ENIAC judgment; the ES EVM decision) live in prose.
  • Textiles: maybe a fifth. Some mills are items; most are not. The dates that exist are patent dates, which this exercise showed are the exact wrong dates.
  • Gunpowder: essentially nothing. Every hop on that map came out of a historian’s argument — Needham, Partington, Chase, Andrade. There is no table anywhere. It is a book.

So the pipeline shape is not “one Wikidata sweep”. It is a small hand-curated spine — origin, unit, claim, and the dozen hops that carry the story — with per-diffusion adapters that can bulk-fill where a real catalogue happens to exist. Printing gets an ISTC adapter and 250 extra dots. Gunpowder never gets one and should not pretend otherwise. Which means the honest engineering answer is: build the shape and the renderer, hand-author ten diffusions, and treat automated fill as a bonus for the two or three where a catalogue exists. Twenty well-authored diffusions is roughly one focused week of a historian’s time; it is also, by a distance, the most defensible content on this page.

One more, found by accident: the corpus’s own printing spread starts at 1439 in Mainz, and industrial-revolution starts at 1760 “Lancashire outward”. The weight curves encode a claim about where these things began. Put Kaifeng 1040 and Cheongju 1377 on the map and the first frame of the scrubber contradicts the corpus. That has to be resolved in the data, not in the UI.

Three questions only you can answer

Each one changes the schema, not the styling. None of them can be deferred past the first ETL run.

  1. What counts as arrival — and is it one answer, or one per diffusion?

    First contact, first local production, first sustained production, or mass adoption. They are decades apart and they disagree about who gets to be first. Printing “arrives” in Istanbul in 1493 or in 1727 depending on whether you mean the machine or the permission. Computing arrives in India in 1955 (a machine at the Indian Statistical Institute) or 1985 (an industry in Bangalore). Gunpowder “arrives” in Mexico in 1519 pointed at the people it arrives among, which is not adoption by anybody. This prototype answers it per diffusion, in a claim field shown to the reader on every map. That is a real position and you may hate it — the alternative is one global rule, which would make three of these four maps wrong.

  2. When does a diffusion stop being about places — and does the map admit it?

    Computing’s unit changes three times: a machine at a place, then a component in a supply chain, then a protocol on a wire. After Mosaic in 1993 there is no next city. Printing’s equivalent moment is the steam press; the loom’s is the container ship. Do these maps stop and say why — which is what this prototype does — or do they change what a dot means halfway through and hope nobody notices? Related and unavoidable: is a diffusion ever finished, and does a place ever lose a technology? Four faded rows say yes, and nothing in the existing model can hold that.

  3. Is this a new object beside spreads, or is it what spreads should have been?

    A spread today is a weight curve plus a moving blob: when and how big, hand-tuned. A diffusion is where-next, and it can generate the blob — a footprint is derivable from arrivals; the reverse is not. So the cheap answer is that footprint becomes computed and hops becomes the source of truth, which also fixes the 1439-Mainz problem above. The expensive question underneath it: who signs the arcs? The dots can be sourced. Every arc on these four maps is a scholarly judgement, and publishing them under the app’s name — with no citation field in the shape yet — is the app making historical claims rather than displaying them. If the answer is “we cite”, the shape needs a src on every hop before the first ETL run, not after.
