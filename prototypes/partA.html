Your core vision: a map of time with level-of-detail. Zoomed out you see only what matters most; scroll to zoom in and smaller events fade in, exactly like streets appearing on Google Maps. Now with a visual grammar — shape says what kind of thing it is, color says what it’s about.

Categories:
Two orthogonal axes. Shape = what kind of thing (a moment, a stretch of time, a life, a territory, an era). Color = which domain it belongs to. That split is what lets one person sit in several domains at once — the question your Notion notes got stuck on. Click any event to open it on Wikipedia.

Empires as flowing ribbons whose thickness is their weight in the world — and lineage as forks: watch the Roman Empire split into West and East, the East run on for a thousand years as Byzantium, and the Ottomans absorb it. This is the 1931 Histomap idea, rebuilt with real lineage links. Hover any ribbon to light up its whole ancestry.

Regions:

Your third take, built: latitude × longitude × time as one solid block. The map is a horizontal slice through it, the core sample is a vertical drill, the timeline is its shadow on the wall. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. Trace an empire to watch it rise as a column through the block.

Trace:
Reading the block: each horizontal sheet is a world map at one date, stacked oldest at the bottom. A territory that persists becomes a column; an empire that grows becomes a cone; a conquest is where one column swallows another.

The map asks “fix a moment, show me everywhere.” The core sample asks the opposite: fix a place, show me every moment. Like a geological drill — newest on top, dig down into the past. Click the world map, or pick a famous drill site:

Braided rivers of ideas

Your own Notion sketch, working: beliefs as streams that fork at schisms and occasionally merge. Christianity splits at 1054 and again at 1517; Islam at 632. The same engine as the empire flow — because a schism and an imperial partition are the same shape of event.

Thickness is an editorial estimate of reach, not a measurement of adherents.

Information horizon

The most novel idea on the list: not what happened, but what a person standing here could possibly have known yet. News moved at the speed of a horse, then a ship, then a telegraph. Pick a city and a year — the rings are how far news had travelled, and the feed on the right is the world still in transit toward you.
